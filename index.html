1961–1972 Lunar missions

Apollo and the first Moon landings

In 1961 President Kennedy set the goal of landing a person on the Moon. Eleven years later NASA had completed six crewed landings, and the program is still studied as a model of large-scale systems engineering.

Apollo 11, July 20, 1969

Lunar orbit rendezvous

The engineering problem

Early plans called for one large vehicle that flew to the Moon, landed and flew home. NASA chose lunar orbit rendezvous instead. A Command and Service Module stayed in lunar orbit while a small two-stage Lunar Module carried two astronauts to the surface and back. Landing less mass on the Moon meant the Saturn V could be a size that was actually buildable.

Navigation depended on the Apollo Guidance Computer, one of the first computers built with integrated circuits. It handled guidance for the trip to the Moon and for the landing itself.

Apollo 13

In April 1970 an oxygen tank failed in the Apollo 13 Service Module. The crew used the Lunar Module as a lifeboat and returned safely to Earth. The mission shows why engineers design spacecraft with backups and why teams on the ground rehearse failures.

1967–today Rockets

Launch vehicles

Every mission starts with a rocket. NASA's three largest crewed launch systems all solve the same problem, lifting heavy payloads out of Earth's gravity, in different ways.

Saturn V

Saturn V had three stages. The first stage burned kerosene and liquid oxygen in five F-1 engines, and each stage was dropped once its propellant ran out. It flew 13 times between 1967 and 1973 and launched every crewed lunar mission.

Space Shuttle

The Shuttle flew 135 missions. Its orbiter and two solid rocket boosters were reused, while the large external tank was not. Two accidents, Challenger in 1986 and Columbia in 2003, cost 14 lives and led to major changes in safety reviews and engineering oversight.

Space Launch System

SLS reuses Shuttle-era hardware, including RS-25 engines and a booster design based on the Shuttle's, inside a new expendable rocket. Comparing the three shows a recurring trade-off: reuse can lower the hardware cost per flight in principle, but it adds weight, complexity and refurbishment work.

2022–today Lunar missions

Artemis

Artemis is NASA's program to return people to the Moon and prepare for longer stays. Its first two flights tested the rocket and spacecraft that later missions depend on.

The heat shield

Returning from the Moon, the Orion capsule hits the atmosphere at about 25,000 mph (11 km/s), faster than a spacecraft coming home from low Earth orbit. Artemis I tested the heat shield in flight. Inspections afterward found it lost more material than predicted, and NASA investigated this before putting a crew on board.

Artemis II

Artemis II carried four astronauts: Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency. The crew flew around the Moon without landing and splashed down in the Pacific Ocean off San Diego, which tested Orion's life support, navigation and recovery with people aboard.

What comes next

Later missions aim to land astronauts near the lunar south pole using commercial landers, with SpaceX and Blue Origin developing the Human Landing Systems. Schedules have changed several times, so check NASA's Artemis pages for the current plan.

1971–today Rovers

Rovers on the Moon and Mars

A rover reaches places a lander cannot. NASA's Mars rovers have grown from a robot the size of a microwave oven to a car-sized laboratory.

The sky crane

Curiosity was too heavy for airbags. Engineers lowered it to the surface on cables from a hovering rocket-powered descent stage, then flew the stage away after touchdown. Perseverance used the same method and added terrain-relative navigation, which compares camera images with onboard maps during descent to steer away from hazards.

Power

Curiosity and Perseverance run on a radioisotope power system that turns heat from decaying plutonium-238 into electricity. Unlike solar panels, it keeps working at night, in winter and through dust storms. Opportunity, built to last 90 Martian days, ran on solar power for more than 14 years before a global dust storm ended contact in 2018.

Beyond driving

Perseverance seals rock samples in tubes for a possible return to Earth, and how and when that happens is still being decided. It also carried the MOXIE experiment, which made oxygen from the carbon dioxide in the Martian air. Ingenuity, a 1.8 kg helicopter that traveled with Perseverance, made the first powered, controlled flight on another planet on April 19, 2021, and flew 72 times before its mission ended in January 2024.

On the Moon

The battery-powered Lunar Roving Vehicle flew on Apollo 15, 16 and 17. It folded up to fit inside the Lunar Module and gave astronauts a much longer reach than walking.
